\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Sampling bearing vibration below the Nyquist rate by pure decimation brings the fault resonance into
a narrow band, and with it whatever impulsive content lies in the folded zones. On three public
benchmarks, that content decided whether blind band selection survived the rate reduction.
\begin{itemize}
  \item On Ottawa, spikes of one sample, healthy records included, captured the kurtogram under
  decimation. Removing isolated outliers in the decimated stream, a three-point median or a lower
  search ceiling avoided the capture at $4\times$ without access to the full rate.
  \item On Paderborn, the kurtogram was captured before any decimation. A decision restricted to
  the fault orders, with a threshold from healthy bearings, recovered much of what the blind
  pipeline missed, at every rate. An anti-alias filter before decimation did better than pure
  decimation, because the diagnostic content lay in the base band.
  \item Learned sampling masks did not outperform trained uniform decimation or random masks on the
  benchmark that could detect a gain of 5 points. The decoder that the masks shared carried the
  margin over the classical pipeline.
\end{itemize}

Three practices follow for a node that samples below the Nyquist rate. Before choosing between pure
decimation and filtering, measure the accuracy by Nyquist zone on records with known faults. After
decimation, protect the band choice or the decision, since the protection that the anti-alias filter
gives at full rate is gone. Before training learned acquisition on a benchmark, check that the
benchmark can detect a gain. The results model an ideal sampler. The bandwidth of a real
track-and-hold, which decides what folds, has to be measured on the target converter.
